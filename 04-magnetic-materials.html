01 · The Biot-Savart Law

Build a magnetic field, one tiny current-piece at a time.

Just like Coulomb's Law describes the field from a single static point of charge, the Biot-Savart Law describes the field from a single tiny piece of current-carrying wire — then you add up every piece along the whole wire to get the total field. The one big difference: it's built from a cross product, so the contribution points perpendicular to both the wire and the line to your observation point, governed by the right-hand rule.

Sweep θ around to 0° or 180° — directly ahead of, or behind, the current element — and the field contribution drops to exactly zero. Biot-Savart's cross product only "sees" the part of r that's sideways to the current; nothing straight ahead ever gets a contribution from that particular piece of wire.
What's the Biot-Savart field contribution from a straight current element at a point lying exactly along the wire's own axis (directly ahead of the current)?
dl × r̂ = |dl||r̂|sin(φ), where φ is the angle between the wire element and the line to the point. Straight ahead, φ=0, sin(0)=0 — no contribution from that segment, no matter how much current it carries.
02 · How Materials Respond to a Field

Every material reacts to a magnetic field. Almost none of them agree on how.

Diamagnetism is present, to some degree, in every material that has ever existed — an applied field induces tiny opposing circulation in each atom's electron cloud, giving a faint, universal repulsion. It's usually so weak it's swamped by other effects, except in special cases like bismuth, water, and superconductors (which are perfect diamagnets, strong enough to levitate a magnet).

Paramagnetism shows up in materials whose atoms already carry a small built-in magnetic moment (from unpaired electron spins) that points randomly in the absence of a field, canceling out on average. Apply a field, and those moments partially tip toward alignment — a weak attraction, constantly fighting against thermal chaos trying to randomize them again.

Ferromagnetism is paramagnetism's much louder cousin: neighboring atomic moments lock onto each other through the quantum-mechanical "exchange interaction," forming self-aligned clusters called domains even with no field applied — the same domains from Chapter 1.3. An external field doesn't have to create alignment from scratch — it just has to grow the favorably-aligned domains at the expense of the others, giving strong attraction that can persist after the field is removed.

03 · Curie's Law & the Curie Temperature

Heat scrambles alignment. Cool it, and order returns.

For paramagnets, Curie's Law makes the temperature dependence explicit: χ = C/T — susceptibility falls off inversely with absolute temperature, because hotter atoms jostle around more violently, randomizing alignment that a field is trying to enforce.

Ferromagnets play the same game, but with a dramatic threshold: below the Curie temperature Tc, the exchange interaction is strong enough to keep domains locked in alignment despite thermal jostling. Push the temperature above Tc (770°C for iron), and thermal chaos finally overwhelms the exchange interaction — domains scramble, spontaneous magnetization vanishes, and the material becomes an ordinary paramagnet until it cools back down.

04 · Permeability

Same coil, same current — a very different field, depending what's inside it.

Permeability, μ = μ₀μᵣ, measures how strongly a material "welcomes" magnetic field lines compared to vacuum. Wind a coil around an iron or ferrite core instead of air, and the internal field B = μH gets amplified by that core's μᵣ — sometimes by a factor of thousands — for the exact same current. This is precisely why real inductors and transformers are built around iron or ferrite cores instead of empty air.

Same wire, same winding, same current — swapping the core material is the single easiest lever for controlling how strong (and how large) an inductor or transformer needs to be for a given job.
For the same coil and the same current, replacing an air core with an iron core (μᵣ in the thousands) does what to the internal magnetic field B?
B = μH = μ₀μᵣH — for fixed current (and therefore fixed H), swapping in a high-μᵣ core multiplies the internal field almost directly by μᵣ. This is the entire reason transformer and inductor cores exist.
