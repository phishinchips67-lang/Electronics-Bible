CHAPTER 2.2 · FROM ROCK TO RADIO

Same component.
Two completely different jobs.

A resistor genuinely doesn't care whether it's fed DC or AC — but almost nothing else in a circuit can say that. Capacitors, inductors, diodes, transistors, and op-amps all behave in fundamentally different ways depending on whether the signal sits still or wiggles.

01 · Every Component, AC vs. DC

Resistance is one number. Impedance is a whole relationship.

Under steady DC, "resistance" is the whole story: V = IR, full stop. Under AC, current has to fight not just resistance but also reactance — an opposition that depends on frequency and shifts current out of phase with voltage. Together they form impedance, Z, the AC generalization of resistance.

X_C = 12πfC     X_L = 2πfL capacitive reactance falls with frequency · inductive reactance rises with frequency

DC: a simple one-way gate — conducts above ~0.7V forward, blocks reverse. AC: used as a rectifier, chopping an alternating signal down to one polarity, minus a fixed voltage bite for the forward drop. (2.3 builds a full rectifier circuit from exactly this behavior.)

AC Input
Rectified Output
At 3V amplitude, the 0.7V forward drop is a small bite out of a big signal. Drag the amplitude down toward 0.7V and watch the "bite" become the whole story — small AC signals get badly distorted by simple diode rectification.

DC: sets the bias point — where on the device's transfer curve it's sitting at rest (cutoff, active/linear, or saturation). AC: a small signal riding on top of that bias point gets amplified according to the local steepness of the curve right there — which is exactly why bias point placement is most of the art of amplifier design.

Bias sits mid-curve, where the slope is steepest — a small input wiggle here produces the biggest, cleanest output swing. This is a linear amplifier's sweet spot.

DC: a precision comparison/amplification tool — the main things that matter are offset voltage and bias currents. AC: gain rolls off past the gain-bandwidth product, and fast, large swings can outrun the op-amp's slew rate — the fastest it can physically change its output — turning a clean sine into a distorted, triangle-ish wave.

Requested signal
Actual output
At low frequency, the output tracks the request cleanly. Push the frequency up and the amplifier simply can't change its output fast enough — the sine collapses toward a triangle wave. This is slew-rate limiting, a real and common op-amp failure mode.
A capacitor is connected across a steady DC source and given plenty of time to settle. What impedance does it now present to further current flow?
This is 1.1's steady-state result restated in impedance language: X_C = 1/(2πfC) → as f → 0 (DC), X_C → ∞. A charged capacitor at DC is an open circuit.
Why does an op-amp's clean sine output degrade toward a triangle wave at high frequency and large amplitude?
A sine wave's steepest slope is proportional to amplitude × frequency. Push either high enough and you exceed the physical maximum dV/dt the output stage can produce — the classic signature is a triangle-shaped output instead of a smooth curve.
