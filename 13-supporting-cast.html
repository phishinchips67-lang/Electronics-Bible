CHAPTER 3.4 · FROM ROCK TO RADIO

The parts that show up
in literally every project.

None of these five are exotic — they're the bolts and hinges of real circuits, the parts you'll reach for constantly once you start building. (Transformers already got their own chapter — 2.3.)

RELAY · CRYSTAL · POTENTIOMETER/LDR/THERMISTOR · OPTOCOUPLER · REGULATOR

01 · The Relay

An electromagnet that flips a mechanical switch.

A relay lets a weak, safe signal (like 5V from a microcontroller) control a strong, dangerous circuit (like 230V mains for a fan or a light) without ever touching it electrically. A small coil, energized by the weak signal, becomes an electromagnet (1.3) that physically yanks a metal switch closed on a completely separate, isolated circuit.

Coil Energizes → Switch Snaps
Coil energized: the electromagnet pulls the arm up to the NO (Normally Open) contact, completing the mains circuit — and the two sides never touch electrically, only mechanically.
02 · The Quartz Crystal

A metronome made of vibrating rock.

Squeeze a quartz crystal and it generates a tiny voltage; apply a voltage and it physically deforms slightly — this is the piezoelectric effect. Cut to the right shape, a quartz crystal vibrates at an incredibly precise, stable frequency (like 32,768 Hz in a watch — see 6.5) when zapped with electricity, giving every microcontroller and clock a rock-steady "tick" to count.

Precision Oscillation
Real crystals oscillate millions of times per second and barely drift — that stability is why your laptop's clock doesn't gain or lose minutes over months, unlike a plain resistor-capacitor timer.
03 · Variable Resistors

Three resistors that change on purpose.

All three of these are "just" resistors whose value changes — by your hand (potentiometer), by light (LDR), or by heat (thermistor). Same Ohm's Law from 1.1, just with a knob that turns the resistance itself into the input signal.

NTC = Negative Temperature Coefficient — hotter means less resistance. This is exactly how a laptop or phone measures internal temperature and decides when to spin up a fan.
04 · The Optocoupler

A wire made of light instead of copper.

An optocoupler packs an LED and a light-sensitive transistor into one chip, facing each other but electrically isolated. Signal goes in as current, gets converted to light, crosses a tiny gap, and gets converted back to current on the other side — like shouting a message across a soundproof glass wall instead of passing a note through it. This protects sensitive low-voltage electronics from dangerous voltage spikes on the other side.

Signal Crosses as Light
Signal is crossing as light. Notice: there is no copper connection at all between the left side and the right side — just a beam.
05 · The Voltage Regulator

A pressure valve that outputs the same voltage no matter what you feed it.

A linear regulator like the classic 7805 takes a messy, higher input voltage and outputs a rock-steady 5V, burning off the excess as heat — like a pressure-reducing valve on a water main that always outputs the same pressure whether the street supply is high or low. Simple, reliable, a little wasteful (2.3's buck converter is the efficient alternative), and in nearly every hobby project ever built.

Output stays locked at 5V regardless of input, as long as input stays above ~7V. All the extra voltage becomes heat in the regulator's metal tab — this is why they need heatsinks in real designs.
Quick Check
Your 7805 is fed 20V instead of 9V, same 5V output. What happens?
Right — the regulator burns off whatever's above 5V as heat, so a bigger gap between input and output means more wasted power and more heat to manage.
