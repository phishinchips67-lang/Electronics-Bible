CHAPTER 2.3 · FROM ROCK TO RADIO

Four machines. Four ways
to convert electricity.

A transformer changes AC voltage. A rectifier turns AC into DC. A converter changes DC voltage. An inverter turns DC back into AC. That's the entire map — every power supply, charger, and drive on Earth is some combination of these four.

01 · Transformers — AC → AC

Two coils that never touch, sharing a story through a changing field.

A transformer is two coils wound around a shared core, with no direct electrical connection between them. AC current in the primary coil creates a changing flux (1.5's Faraday's Law); the high-permeability core (1.4) channels that flux efficiently over to the secondary coil, where the same changing flux induces its own EMF. A transformer's primary is connected directly to a steady DC voltage source and left alone. What happens at the secondary, once things settle?
Only a *changing* flux induces anything (1.5). DC gives you one brief transient spike as flux first ramps up, then nothing — meanwhile the primary, with no back-EMF from a changing flux to restrain it, behaves like a low-resistance coil sitting straight across the source, risking overheating or core saturation. Transformers are fundamentally AC-only devices.
02 · Rectifiers — AC → DC

Diodes as one-way valves, letting only the "forward" half through.

A rectifier turns AC into (rough) DC using nothing but diodes' one-way behavior from 2.2. The simplest version, a half-wave rectifier, uses a single diode: the positive half of each cycle passes through, the negative half is simply blocked, leaving a pulsing output with long gaps at zero. A full-wave bridge rectifier uses four diodes arranged so that both halves of the AC cycle get routed the same way through the load — the negative half isn't discarded, it's flipped positive — giving a much smoother, gap-free pulsing DC that's far easier to filter into something steady with a capacitor afterward.

Half-Wave vs. Full-Wave Bridge
Half-wave: a single diode in series with the load conducts only during the positive half — the negative half is thrown away entirely, leaving flat gaps at zero. Watch the flow-dashes freeze completely during the blocked half.
Why does a full-wave bridge rectifier produce a "smoother" DC output than a half-wave rectifier, before any filtering capacitor is added?
Half-wave throws away half of every cycle. Full-wave bridge rectification flips the negative half positive instead of discarding it, so the output pulses twice as often with no dead gaps — much less filtering work needed to smooth it into steady DC.
03 · DC-DC Converters — DC → DC

Gearing voltage down without wasting the difference as heat.

Say a battery sits at 300–800V, but the accessories on the same system — lights, displays, door locks — all run on a boring 12V rail. A linear regulator (3.4) could technically do it, but would incinerate itself dissipating hundreds of volts as pure heat. Instead, a buck converter switches the high voltage rapidly on and off with a transistor, then uses an inductor and capacitor (1.1) to smooth those chopped pulses into a clean, lower, steady DC voltage — like a bicycle gear that trades speed for torque without burning off the difference as friction.

Vout = Vin × duty cycle. Whatever the battery's state of charge is doing to its voltage, the converter's duty cycle constantly adjusts to keep the 12V rail rock steady.
Boost converters use the same three parts (switch, inductor, capacitor) rearranged to do the reverse — step a lower DC voltage up — by exploiting the inductor's own Chapter 1.1 trick: it fights any sudden drop in current by releasing a brief high-voltage kick, which a diode then captures onto the output capacitor.
04 · Inverters — DC → AC

Four switches, flipped in the right order, fake an AC wave from pure DC.

An inverter turns DC into AC using four switches (in practice, the MOSFETs or IGBTs from 2.2) arranged in an H-bridge around the load. Closing one diagonal pair sends current one way through the load; closing the other diagonal pair reverses it. From the battery's perspective, it's still just DC — but the load sees current flipping back and forth, which is AC, however crude.

H-Bridge Switching
A plain square wave is genuinely AC — current really does reverse — but it's a crude sine substitute, rich in unwanted harmonics. PWM instead switches rapidly with a varying pulse width; feed that through a small output filter (an inductor and capacitor, straight out of 2.2) and what comes out the other side is a close approximation of a smooth sine wave — this is how modern "pure sine wave" inverters work.
What's the core advantage of PWM switching in an inverter, compared to a plain on/off square wave?
A square wave is stuck alternating between two fixed levels. PWM's varying duty cycle gives a filter something much closer to a genuine sine shape to work with, which is exactly why higher-quality inverters use it instead of plain square-wave switching.
