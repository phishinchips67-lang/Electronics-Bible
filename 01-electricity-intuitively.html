01 · The Big Picture

Electricity is just water in a very impatient pipe.

Before any formulas: three words you'll hear constantly are voltage, current, and resistance. Here's the only mental model you actually need — a water tower feeding a pipe. Voltage is the pressure pushing the water. Current is how much water is actually flowing past a point per second. Resistance is how narrow the pipe is — narrow the pipe, and less water gets through for the same push.

Live Model
This is Ohm's Law already. More pressure (voltage) → more flow (current). A narrower valve (resistance) → less flow for the same pressure. That's it. That's the whole law: I = VR.

The Real Definitions

The water analogy is a mental model. Here's what these units actually mean, in real physics — an engineer's version, not just a picture:

1 Coulomb
≈ 6.24×10¹⁸ electrons
1 Ampere
1 coulomb / second
1 Volt
1 joule / coulomb
1 Watt
1 joule / second

A volt isn't really "pressure" — it's energy per unit of charge. A 9V battery hands every coulomb that passes through it 9 joules to spend. An amp isn't really "flow rate" — it's literally how many coulombs go past per second. The water analogy gets the behavior right; this is what's actually happening underneath it.

03 · The Resistor

The component whose entire job is to say "not so fast."

A resistor doesn't block current — it converts some of the electrical energy into heat as electrons shove past a material that doesn't want to carry them easily, like runners squeezing through a narrow doorway. More resistance = more shoving = more heat = less current gets through.

Why it heats up

Inside that tan body is carbon or a thin metal film — a material with just enough atoms in the way that electrons constantly bump into them. Every bump releases a tiny bit of heat. Multiply that by billions of electrons per second, and a resistor pushed too hard (see the bulb in Section 2) literally burns.

Power rule: P = I² × R. Double the current through a resistor and it dissipates 4× the heat, not 2×.
Quick Check
You double the resistance in a circuit but keep voltage the same. What happens to current?
Right — I = V/R. Double the denominator, halve the answer. This is why series resistors always reduce current.
04 · One Path, Or Many

Wire two resistors two different ways, and they stop behaving alike.

Chain two resistors one after another and every electron must pass through both — that's series. Wire them side by side between the same two points and each electron only has to pick one — that's parallel. Same two resistors, two completely different circuits, two completely different sets of rules.

Quick Check
You wire two identical resistors in parallel instead of series. What happens to the total resistance?
Right — for two equal resistors in parallel, R = (R×R)/(R+R) = R/2. Every extra parallel path gives current an easier way through, so total resistance always drops.
05 · The Capacitor

A tiny water tank that hates being empty or full.

A capacitor stores charge between two metal plates. Connect it to a voltage source and it fills up — fast at first, then slower as it gets close to full, exactly like filling a tank against back-pressure. Disconnect the source and give it a path, and it drains the same way. It never switches instantly — that lag is the entire reason capacitors exist in circuits (smoothing, timing, filtering).

Charge / Discharge
This delay is called the RC time constant. Bigger resistor or bigger capacitor = slower fill. Engineers use this on purpose to build timers and smooth out noisy power.
06 · The Inductor

A coil with the momentum of a flywheel.

An inductor is just a coil of wire, but current through a coil creates a magnetic field — and that field resists any change in current, the same way a heavy flywheel resists being sped up or slowed down. Try to switch current on instantly and the inductor fights it, ramping up gradually. Try to switch it off instantly and it fights that too, sometimes hard enough to spark.

Flywheel Model
Watch the flywheel — it doesn't jump to speed. It winds up. That ramp-up time is the inductor "charging" its magnetic field, and it's why inductors are used in power supplies to smooth sudden current spikes.
