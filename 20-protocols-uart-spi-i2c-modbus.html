CHAPTER 4.7 · FROM ROCK TO RADIO

How two chips agree
to even start talking.

Four protocols, four completely different philosophies for the same basic problem: getting bits from one chip to another reliably, with as few wires as the job allows.

SERIAL vs PARALLEL · UART · SPI · I2C · MODBUS
Serial vs Parallel UART SPI I2C Wiring Cost Modbus
01 · Serial vs Parallel

One lane, taking turns — vs many lanes, all at once.

Parallel sends every bit of a byte at the same instant, one wire per bit — an 8-lane highway where 8 cars arrive together. Serial sends bits one after another on a single wire — one car at a time on one lane. Parallel looks faster on paper, but wires drift out of sync over distance and cost multiplies with every added line — which is exactly why USB, SATA, and PCIe all quietly switched from parallel to blazing-fast serial as speeds increased.

Parallel: all 8 bits land simultaneously in one clock tick — fast, but needs 8 perfectly matched wires that must all arrive at exactly the same moment.
02 · UART (Serial, No Shared Clock)

Two chips agreeing on a rhythm ahead of time.

UART is the simplest serial protocol: just two wires (TX and RX), no shared clock line at all. Instead, both sides agree in advance on a speed (the baud rate) — two people agreeing to speak at exactly one word per second before a call with a bad connection. Each byte is wrapped in a start bit (line drops low) and a stop bit (line returns high) so the receiver knows exactly when a byte begins and ends.

Byte Framing
START (always 0) → 8 data bits → STOP (always 1). No clock wire needed — as long as both chips agreed on the same baud rate beforehand, timing alone carries the message.
Two UART devices are wired correctly, but one is configured for 9600 baud and the other for 115200 baud. What happens?
This is the direct cost of UART's simplicity — with no shared clock, both ends are trusting their own independent timing entirely. A baud mismatch is one of the most common real-world "why is my serial monitor showing gibberish" bugs.
03 · SPI

A shared clock buys speed, at the cost of a wire per device.

SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface) adds a dedicated clock line (SCLK) that the master drives — no baud-rate-guessing needed, every bit is sampled exactly on a clock edge. Data flows on two more wires: MOSI (Master Out, Slave In) and MISO (Master In, Slave Out) — both directions at once, every single clock pulse, making SPI genuinely full-duplex. A separate CS (Chip Select) wire per device says who's currently being talked to; pull a device's CS low, and only it listens.

One Byte, Full Duplex
SHIFT REGISTER — WHAT THE MASTER HAS RECEIVED SO FAR
Both bytes travel simultaneously, on the same 8 clock pulses — that's the "full duplex" part. Hit the button to watch the master's shift register fill in with the MISO byte, bit by bit.
On SPI, if the master wants to talk to a third device while two others are also on the same clock/MOSI/MISO bus, what does it do?
SPI has no addressing scheme built into the data itself — a device's own dedicated CS wire is entirely how it's selected, which is exactly why SPI needs one extra wire per device (see the wiring-cost demo below).
04 · I2C

Every device shares two wires — and answers only to its own name.

I2C squeezes everything onto just two wires — SDA (data) and SCL (clock) — shared by every device on the bus, using the open-drain wiring from 4.4 so nobody ever electrically fights. A transaction starts with a START condition (SDA drops while SCL stays high — a deliberate rule-break that can't occur mid-byte, so it's unmistakable), followed by a 7-bit address plus a read/write bit, then an ACK bit — only the device whose address matches pulls SDA low to acknowledge; everyone else stays silent.

Address, Then Only One Device Answers
Pick a target address and hit Start — watch the START condition, then the address bits, then only the matching device pull SDA low for ACK. The rest of the bus stays passive, exactly as open-drain wiring (4.4) allows.
Why can I2C's START condition (SDA dropping while SCL is held high) never be confused with an ordinary data bit?
This is a clever, deliberate rule violation used as a signal — since it can never legitimately happen mid-byte, every device on the bus instantly and unambiguously recognizes "a new transaction is starting" the moment they see it.

This gap only widens as you add devices — exactly why I2C dominates for wiring up a handful of cheap sensors on a pin-limited chip, even though it's electrically slower than SPI.
Why does I2C need only 2 wires no matter how many devices are on the bus, while SPI needs one extra wire per device?
SPI trades pins for speed and simplicity — the CS line itself says who's being talked to, no addressing logic needed. I2C trades a bit of speed for pin efficiency by giving every device its own address instead.
06 · Modbus

I2C's addressing idea, scaled up to a whole factory floor.

Modbus — the protocol you've likely seen in industrial automation — works on the same core idea as I2C's addressing, but over longer distances: RS-485 (two wires, differential signaling for serious noise immunity) or plain Ethernet. A single master polls each device by address, one at a time, in strict turn; every device hears every request, but only the addressed one responds.

Modbus Poll Simulator
Only the addressed slave responds. Every device on the line hears the request, but ignores it unless the address matches — exactly why you never assign two field devices the same Modbus ID.
