01 · The Diode

A one-way check valve for electricity.

A diode lets current flow in exactly one direction and blocks it in the other — like a check valve in a water pipe that flaps open under forward pressure and slams shut if the flow tries to reverse. Flip the battery around in the circuit below and watch the current simply... stop.

Forward vs Reverse Bias
Forward biased: positive terminal pushes into the triangle side. Electrons flow easily, LED lights up. This is the "normal" way you'd wire a diode.
02 · The Zener Diode

A pressure-relief valve that clamps voltage on purpose.

A normal diode blocked in reverse just... blocks, forever, until it dies. A zener is a diode engineered to break down at a precise reverse voltage and stay there safely — like a pressure relief valve on a boiler that pops open at exactly 5.1 bar and holds the pressure there, no matter how hard you push past it. That's why zeners are used to create a stable reference voltage.

Input
2 V
Output (clamped)
2 V
Below 5.1V: the zener behaves like an ordinary blocked diode — output just follows input.
03 · The BJT Transistor

A tiny valve that controls a much bigger one.

A BJT (Bipolar Junction Transistor) has three legs: base, collector, emitter. A small current into the base controls a much larger current flowing from collector to emitter — like a small pilot valve on a garden tap that, when you nudge it slightly, opens a massive main valve behind it. The ratio between them is called gain (β), often 100–300×.

Base Current → Collector Current
Base current
0.2 mA
Collector current
30 mA
This is amplification. A tiny 0.2mA signal is controlling a 30mA current — 150× bigger. This is exactly how a microphone's weak signal ends up driving a loud speaker.
04 · The MOSFET

A switch flipped by voltage, not current.

A MOSFET also has three legs — gate, drain, source — but works differently from a BJT: the gate needs almost zero current, just a voltage above a threshold, to open a conducting channel between drain and source. Think of it like a motion-sensor door: you don't need to physically push it, just being present (a voltage) is enough to swing it open. This is why MOSFETs dominate modern digital electronics — they waste almost no power just sitting there "listening."

Gate Voltage → Channel
Gate voltage
0.0 V
Gate current
≈ 0 mA
Channel state
OFF
Threshold ≈ 2V. Below it the channel doesn't exist and no current flows, no matter what's on the drain side. Push past it and the channel opens — and notice the gate current stays basically zero the whole time.
05 · BJT vs MOSFET

Same job, two completely different control styles.

Both are switches/amplifiers built from the same three ideas as everything before this — but one is controlled by current, the other by voltage. Here's the practical difference that actually matters when you pick one for a project.

Head to Head
PropertyBJTMOSFET
Controlled byBase currentGate voltage

Rule of thumb: if you're switching something with a microcontroller pin (like an Arduino), reach for a MOSFET — the pin can barely supply a BJT's base current, but it can easily supply the voltage a MOSFET's gate needs.
Quick Check
You want to switch a 12V motor on/off using a Raspberry Pi GPIO pin (which supplies very little current). Which is the better choice?
Right — a GPIO pin struggles to supply the base current a BJT needs for a 12V motor's load, but easily supplies the voltage a MOSFET's gate wants.
