CHAPTER 1.2 · FROM ROCK TO RADIO

Not orbits.
Just charge.

Every circuit you'll ever build traces back to one unexplained fact: some particles push, some pull, and it has nothing to do with them spinning around anything. This is the physics underneath the water analogy — what voltage, "ground," and "ampere" actually are.

01 · What Is Charge, Really?

It was never the orbiting. The charge was always there.

Here's the misconception worth killing first: charge is not something an electron generates by circling a proton, the way spinning a bicycle wheel generates momentum. That mental picture comes from the old Bohr "solar system" atom — a useful cartoon, but wrong in exactly this way. An electron sitting perfectly still, with no proton anywhere nearby, still has exactly the same charge it always had. Motion has nothing to do with it.

So what is charge, then? It's best treated as an intrinsic property of certain particles — as fundamental and un-derived as mass. An electron doesn't "have" mass because it's doing something; it just has mass, permanently, as part of what makes it an electron and not some other particle. Charge works the same way. Every electron in the universe carries exactly −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, every proton carries exactly +1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, full stop — not because of what they're doing, but because of what they are.

Orbiting vs. Frozen Still
Watch the cyan halo, not the motion. That halo represents the electron's field — the region of space it pushes and pulls on. Freeze the orbit completely and the halo doesn't shrink, flicker, or change in any way. Charge is a property the electron carries with it at all times, moving or still.

The other load-bearing fact about charge is that it's quantized — it doesn't come in a smooth, infinitely divisible continuum like pouring water. It comes in fixed-size steps, all whole-number multiples of one elementary unit, e ≈ 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. You can have a charge of −2e or +5e, but never 1.5e. (Quarks, bound permanently inside protons and neutrons, carry fractional charges of e — +⅔e or −⅓e — but the strong force never lets you isolate one to measure alone; every particle you can actually pick up and hold has a whole-number charge.)

Charge is intrinsic — it doesn't require motion to exist, and it can't be created or destroyed, only moved around or (in pair-production/annihilation) converted between particles while the total stays conserved.
02 · Coulomb's Law

Same shape as gravity. Almost incomprehensibly stronger.

Charles-Augustin de Coulomb measured it directly in 1785: the force between two charges is proportional to each charge's size, and falls off with the square of the distance between them.

F = k · |q₁ q₂|r² k = 1 / (4πε₀) ≈ 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C² — ε₀ = permittivity of free space

Same charges (+/+ or −/−) push apart; opposite charges (+/−) pull together. Notice the exponent: double the distance and the force doesn't halve — it drops to a quarter. Triple it, and the force falls to a ninth. This inverse-square shape is exactly the same one gravity uses, which isn't a coincidence — both describe an influence spreading out over the surface of an ever-expanding sphere.

A sense of scale: the reason gravity feels like the dominant force in daily life — even though it's astronomically weaker per particle — is that ordinary matter is almost perfectly electrically neutral, with equal positive and negative charge cancelling out at short range. Strip that cancellation away, even slightly, and electric forces overwhelm gravity instantly. A single bolt of lightning is charge imbalance winning.
You slide q₂ so the distance r doubles, changing nothing else. What happens to the force?
F ∝ 1/r² — an inverse-square law. Doubling r means dividing the force by 2² = 4, regardless of how big or small the charges are.

03 · The Electric Field

Force, pre-divided down to a property of empty space.

Instead of asking "what force does charge A put on charge B," it's more powerful to split the question in two. First: what does charge A do to the space around it, regardless of who or what ever shows up? That's the electric field, E. Second, whenever some other charge q happens to sit at a point in that space, the force on it is simply F = qE at that location.

E = F / qfield = force per unit of (positive) test charge, measured in N/C — identical to V/m

That division by q is the whole trick. Bring a huge test charge into the field and it feels a huge force; bring a tiny one and it feels a tiny force — but divide either force by its own charge, and you get back the exact same number both times. That number belongs to the source charge and the location, not to whatever you used to probe it. It's the same logic as asking "how steep is this hill" instead of "how hard did that specific rock roll" — steepness is a property of the hill; rolling force depends on the rock too.

Why define the field as E = F/q instead of just talking about the force directly?
Force always depends on two charges. Dividing by the test charge's own size leaves behind a quantity that depends only on the source charge and where you're standing — reusable for any test charge you might bring in next.
04 · Electric Potential

Height, but for charge instead of gravity.

If the field E is like the steepness of a hill at each point, the electric potential V is the height itself. It's defined as potential energy per unit charge, V = U/q, and for a point charge it works out to V = kQ/r — highest right next to a positive charge, falling off as you move away. Drop a positive test charge anywhere near a positive source and, exactly like a ball on a real hill, it naturally accelerates away, toward lower potential — because that's the direction that releases stored energy.

V = k · Qr     U = qV V in volts (joules per coulomb) — a scalar, no direction, unlike E

A positive test charge is released near a negative source charge. Which way does it move, and why?
Near a negative charge, V is deeply negative and climbs toward zero as you move away — so the "downhill" direction (toward lower V) is actually inward, toward the source. That's the potential-energy picture of attraction.
05 · Gauss's Law

Count what's inside. Ignore everything about the shape.

Gauss's Law says the total electric flux — loosely, "how much field pokes through" — leaving any closed surface depends only on the net charge sealed up inside it. Not the surface's shape, not its size, not anything about charges sitting outside. Picture a running tap sealed inside a balloon: however you deform that balloon — sphere, potato, star-shaped — the total flow rate measured leaving its surface is identical, because it's all coming from one place: the tap.

∮ E · dA = Qencε₀total flux through any closed surface = enclosed charge ÷ permittivity of free space

The payoff: for a highly symmetric charge distribution — a sphere, an infinite line, an infinite sheet — you can choose a Gaussian surface that matches the symmetry, and E pops out algebraically in one line, with zero calculus. It's the reason you can quote the field of a charged sphere or a charged plate from memory instead of re-deriving it from Coulomb's Law every time.

06 · Electrical Ground

There's no such thing as an absolute voltage — only a difference.

You can't measure "the voltage" at a single point any more than you can measure "the altitude" of a single point without agreeing on sea level first. Every voltmeter reading is secretly a comparison between two probes. Ground is simply the node a circuit's designer picks to call 0V — after that, every other point can be labeled with one number instead of always saying "12V above the other one."

Crucially, that reference node does not need to be connected to the physical planet to deserve the name. A 9V battery circuit sitting in your hand, completely isolated from any wire touching the dirt outside, still has a perfectly good "ground" — it's just the battery's negative terminal. This is called a floating ground, and it's just as legitimate internally as one wired to an actual earth rod. "Earth ground" is a separate, more specific idea covered next.

True or false: a circuit's "ground" must always be physically wired to the actual planet Earth.
A battery-powered, fully isolated circuit (think: a phone, a drone, a car's 12V system) has a perfectly good internal ground with zero physical connection to soil. "Earth ground" — an actual rod in the dirt — is a specific extra feature added for safety, not a requirement for the concept of ground itself.
07 · Earth Ground & Fault Protection

The planet as an emergency exit for stray current.

Physical earth ground earns its special status from one fact: the Earth is such a colossally large conductor that dumping charge into it, or pulling charge out, barely nudges its potential at all. That makes it an extremely stable, universally available 0V — which is why building codes bond the metal chassis of appliances to an actual earth rod through the third prong of a plug.

The real payoff shows up during a fault — say, a frayed hot wire inside a metal-cased appliance brushing against the chassis. Without a ground wire, the chassis itself becomes live at full mains voltage, waiting silently for someone to touch it and become the path to earth themselves. With a ground wire in place, the fault current has an easy, low-resistance route to earth instead — a route so much easier than through a person that it draws a massive current spike, which trips the breaker or blows the fuse in well under a second, cutting power before anyone is ever at risk.

Ground loops, briefly: if two pieces of equipment are grounded at two physically different points, tiny currents already flowing through the earth or building wiring can create a small voltage difference between those two "zeros" — injecting hum or noise into sensitive signal lines. The usual fix is single-point grounding: tying every ground reference back to one common point instead of many scattered ones.
Why does a properly grounded chassis protect a nearby person during a fault, even though they never touch the ground wire themselves?
Current takes the path of least resistance, in proportion — most of it flows through whichever route is easiest. A dedicated ground wire is a far easier route than a human body, and the resulting current spike is exactly what trips the breaker before real danger can develop.
