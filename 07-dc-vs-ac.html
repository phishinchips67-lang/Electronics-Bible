CHAPTER 2.1 · FROM ROCK TO RADIO

Fixed polarity.
Polarity that flips.

DC is a battery: + is always + and − is always −, and electrons drift one way, full stop. AC is the wall socket: the terminals swap which one is + and which is − dozens of times a second, and the electrons never actually go anywhere — they just jiggle back and forth in place.

01 · Fixed vs Switching Polarity

The entire difference, before any math: which way is "forward"?

Direct Current (DC) comes from a source — a battery, a solar panel, a USB port — where one terminal is permanently + and the other is permanently −. Current always flows the same direction around the circuit, for as long as the source lasts.

Alternating Current (AC) comes from a source whose terminals swap which one is + and which is − on a regular schedule — 50 or 60 times a second for mains power. Current doesn't pick a direction and stay there; it reverses in lockstep with the terminals, over and over. Zoom in far enough, and the electrons in an AC wire aren't traveling anywhere at all on average — they're sloshing back and forth around a fixed spot, like water in a shaken bottle rather than water in a river.

This regularly surprises people: in AC, individual charges barely move at all — they jiggle in place, dozens of times a second. What actually races down the wire near the speed of light is the electromagnetic field telling every electron along the whole wire to move together, which is how a lamp can light up "instantly" even though no single electron traveled from the switch to the bulb.
02 · Anatomy of a Sine Wave

Four numbers describe the entire shape.

Mains AC isn't just "reversing" — it reverses smoothly, tracing a sine wave. Four quantities fully describe it: amplitude (the peak height), period T (seconds per full cycle), frequency f = 1/T (cycles per second, in Hertz), and phase (where in the cycle it starts — this matters once you compare two waveforms against each other, which Chapter 2.2 does constantly).

03 · RMS — the "Effective" Value

"120V" and "230V" were never the peak voltage.

Here's a genuinely strange fact: US mains is quoted as 120V, but the sine wave's actual peak is about 170V. That 120V is a Root Mean Square (RMS) value — defined so that an AC source at that RMS voltage delivers exactly the same average heating power to a resistor as a DC source at that same voltage would. For a pure sine wave, the relationship is fixed:

Both bulbs glow identically — same average power, same heat, same light — because the DC voltage was deliberately set to the AC waveform's RMS value. This is the entire reason RMS is the number printed on every appliance and outlet: it's the number that actually tells you how much heating/working power you're getting.
A European outlet is rated 230V. What is the actual peak voltage the wire reaches at the top of each AC cycle?
V_peak = V_RMS × √2 = 230 × 1.414 ≈ 325V. Insulation, spacing, and component voltage ratings all have to be designed around this real peak, not the more familiar RMS number.
04 · Why AC Won the Grid

Not because AC is "better" — because it's easier to change.

Power lost in a transmission wire is Ploss = I²R — it depends only on current and the wire's own resistance, not on the voltage doing the delivering. So for a fixed amount of power, the trick to cutting losses is simple: push the voltage way up, which pushes the current way down for the same delivered power, which crushes I²R losses. The catch is that homes need a safe, low voltage — so something has to step voltage up for the long haul and back down at the destination.

A transformer (Chapter 2.3) does exactly that for AC, cheaply and with no moving parts, using nothing but Chapter 1.5's induction. In the late 1800s "War of the Currents," this was the deciding advantage AC (championed by Westinghouse and Tesla) held over Edison's DC: there was no equally simple, efficient way to transform DC voltage up and down at the time. That's why the grid you plug into today is AC almost everywhere.

This isn't the whole modern story. Power electronics have since gotten good enough that long-distance HVDC (High-Voltage DC) links are now genuinely used for certain jobs — especially very long undersea cables and connecting grids that don't share the same frequency — because DC avoids some AC-specific transmission losses over very long distances. But the everyday grid feeding your outlets is still built on AC, top to bottom, for exactly the historical reason above.
