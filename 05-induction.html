01 · Faraday's Law

A changing flux writes a voltage into any nearby loop.

Flux, Φ = B·A·cos θ, is a measure of how much magnetic field passes through a loop. Faraday's Law says any change in that flux induces an EMF (voltage) around the loop — and critically, it's the rate of change that matters, not the field strength itself. Hold an enormously strong magnet perfectly still inside a coil and you'll measure exactly zero volts. Whip a weak magnet in and out fast, and you'll see a real voltage spike. (You saw a first taste of this in 1.3 — this is the properly stated version.)

Faraday's Law only cares about change. A static field, however strong, produces zero induced EMF — motion (or some other change) is non-negotiable.
02 · Lenz's Law

The induced current always fights back.

Lenz's Law is the minus sign in Faraday's equation, made physically vivid: the induced current always flows in the direction that opposes the change that created it. A magnet approaching a coil induces a current whose own field pushes back, repelling the approach. A magnet retreating induces a current whose field instead pulls it back, resisting the departure. This isn't an extra rule bolted onto Faraday's Law — it's a direct requirement of energy conservation. If induced currents ever assisted the change instead of opposing it, you could build a loop that accelerates itself forever, for free.

Approach vs. Retreat
Push the magnet toward the coil, then pull it away — watch both the current-loop arrow and the force arrow reverse direction between the two cases. Stop moving, and both vanish: no change, no induced current.
A magnet is being pulled away from a coil. Which way does the induced current's own magnetic field act, according to Lenz's Law?
Whatever the change is, the induced effect opposes it. Flux decreasing (magnet leaving) → induced current tries to keep the flux up → that means trying to keep the magnet from leaving, i.e., an attractive pull.
03 · Eddy Currents

Lenz's Law, but the "coil" is just a solid chunk of metal.

A discrete wire loop isn't required for induction — drop a magnet through a solid conductive pipe, and the bulk metal itself hosts countless overlapping circulating currents, called eddy currents, induced by the same Faraday/Lenz mechanism. Those currents dissipate real energy as heat (I²R losses in the metal) and, by Lenz's Law, create a drag force that opposes whatever caused them — the classic demo is a magnet falling dramatically slower through a copper or aluminum tube than through an otherwise identical plastic one, despite copper not being magnetic at all.

Choose a tube material, then drop the magnet. Solid copper — despite being completely non-magnetic — dramatically slows the fall. Slotting the same copper tube lengthwise breaks the closed current loops eddy currents depend on, and the fast fall returns.
Why transformer cores are laminated: a solid iron core sitting in a 50/60 Hz AC field would host large, wasteful eddy currents throughout its bulk, turning good electricity into unwanted heat. Real cores are instead built from thin, individually-insulated sheets stacked together — breaking up the large-scale loops (just like the slotted tube) without weakening the core's magnetic permeability at all. More on this in 2.3.
Why does cutting a slot lengthwise down a conductive tube restore a dropped magnet's fast fall, compared to an identical solid tube?
Eddy currents need an unbroken conductive loop to circulate in. Cut that loop anywhere, and there's no closed path left for them to flow — the braking effect disappears almost entirely, even though it's still the exact same conductive metal.
04 · Hysteresis

Ferromagnetic materials remember where they've been.

Sweep an external field H up and down through a ferromagnetic material and plot the resulting internal field B — it doesn't retrace the same curve. It lags behind, tracing out a loop. Key landmarks: saturation (B stops climbing once nearly all domains are aligned), remanence Bᵣ (the B left behind when H returns to zero — this is what makes a permanent magnet permanent), and coercivity Hc (how much reverse field it takes to erase B back to zero). The loop's enclosed area is real energy, lost as heat, every single cycle.
