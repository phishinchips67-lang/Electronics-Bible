Sampling & Aliasing The Exam Toolkit
01 · Sampling & Aliasing

Sample too slowly and a fast signal lies to you.

A camera shutter clicking once per wagon-wheel rotation makes the wheel look stopped — that's a movie cliché and it's also exactly Shannon's sampling theorem. Sample below twice the signal frequency (the Nyquist rate) and the samples are indistinguishable from a much slower signal: an alias. Your controller can't tell the difference — it just acts on the wrong frequency.

Your plant has fast 40Hz vibration noise and you sample at 50Hz. What should you do before sampling, and why?
50Hz sampling only safely captures signals up to 25Hz (fs/2). That 40Hz vibration will fold down into a false low-frequency signal inside your controller's working range — an anti-aliasing filter has to remove it before the ADC ever sees it.

4 · Deadbeat Control — Worked Pattern

Deadbeat design places every closed-loop pole at z = 0 — the fastest possible response that reaches steady-state in exactly n steps (n = system order) and stays there:

Desired char. poly: φ_d(z) = zⁿ Then apply Ackermann's formula (from 5.2) with A, B replaced by the discrete pair A_d, B_d.

Same algorithm as continuous pole placement — only the target polynomial changes. This is one of the most re-used ideas between 5.2 and 5.3's exams.

5 · Discrete LQR

Minimise a weighted cost over the whole trajectory instead of placing poles by hand:

J = Σ ( x_k' Q x_k + u_k' R u_k ) u_k = -K x_k solves the discrete Riccati equation for K

Q penalises state error, R penalises control effort — exam questions usually give you Q and R and ask you to reason about the resulting trade-off qualitatively, not grind the Riccati equation by hand.

Common exam trap: forgetting that a continuous design that's stable doesn't automatically stay stable after discretisation — always re-check pole locations are inside the unit circle in the z-plane, not the left-half s-plane. This single check is worth free marks that a surprising number of students skip under time pressure.
