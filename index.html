explained.

Not "V = IR, memorize it." You're going to watch voltage, current and resistance happen — drag sliders, flip switches, and see the numbers move because you moved them.

01 · The Big Picture

Electricity is just water in a very impatient pipe.

Before any formulas: three words you'll hear constantly are voltage, current, and resistance. Here's the only mental model you actually need — a water tower feeding a pipe. Voltage is the pressure pushing the water. Current is how much water is actually flowing past a point per second. Resistance is how narrow the pipe is — narrow the pipe, and less water gets through for the same push.

Live Model
This is Ohm's Law already. More pressure (voltage) → more flow (current). A narrower valve (resistance) → less flow for the same pressure. That's it. That's the whole law: I = VR.

Build a real circuit. Break it if you want.

This is an actual working circuit: a battery, a resistor, and a bulb. Drag the sliders and watch the electrons (the little dashes on the wire) speed up or slow down, and the bulb get brighter or dimmer — in real time, because of the math you're changing.

A volt isn't really "pressure" — it's energy per unit of charge. A 9V battery hands every coulomb that passes through it 9 joules to spend. An amp isn't really "flow rate" — it's literally how many coulombs go past per second. The water analogy gets the behavior right; this is what's actually happening underneath it.

03 · The Resistor

The component whose entire job is to say "not so fast."

A resistor doesn't block current — it converts some of the electrical energy into heat as electrons shove past a material that doesn't want to carry them easily, like runners squeezing through a narrow doorway. More resistance = more shoving = more heat = less current gets through.

Color Code Decoder
Reads as
100 Ω
Why it heats up

Inside that tan body is carbon or a thin metal film — a material with just enough atoms in the way that electrons constantly bump into them. Every bump releases a tiny bit of heat. Multiply that by billions of electrons per second, and a resistor pushed too hard (see the bulb in Section 2) literally burns.

Power rule: P = I² × R. Double the current through a resistor and it dissipates 4× the heat, not 2×.
Quick Check
You double the resistance in a circuit but keep voltage the same. What happens to current?
Right — I = V/R. Double the denominator, halve the answer. This is why series resistors always reduce current.
04 · One Path, Or Many

Wire two resistors two different ways, and they stop behaving alike.

Chain two resistors one after another and every electron must pass through both — that's series. Wire them side by side between the same two points and each electron only has to pick one — that's parallel. Same two resistors, two completely different circuits, two completely different sets of rules.

05 · The Capacitor

A tiny water tank that hates being empty or full.

A capacitor stores charge between two metal plates. Connect it to a voltage source and it fills up — fast at first, then slower as it gets close to full, exactly like filling a tank against back-pressure. Disconnect the source and give it a path, and it drains the same way. It never switches instantly — that lag is the entire reason capacitors exist in circuits (smoothing, timing, filtering).

Charge / Discharge
This delay is called the RC time constant. Bigger resistor or bigger capacitor = slower fill. Engineers use this on purpose to build timers and smooth out noisy power.
06 · The Inductor

A coil with the momentum of a flywheel.

An inductor is just a coil of wire, but current through a coil creates a magnetic field — and that field resists any change in current, the same way a heavy flywheel resists being sped up or slowed down. Try to switch current on instantly and the inductor fights it, ramping up gradually. Try to switch it off instantly and it fights that too, sometimes hard enough to spark.

Flywheel Model
Watch the flywheel — it doesn't jump to speed. It winds up. That ramp-up time is the inductor "charging" its magnetic field, and it's why inductors are used in power supplies to smooth sudden current spikes.

CHAPTER 1.2 · FROM ROCK TO RADIO

Not orbits.
Just charge.

Every circuit you'll ever build traces back to one unexplained fact: some particles push, some pull, and it has nothing to do with them spinning around anything. This is the physics underneath the water analogy — what voltage, "ground," and "ampere" actually are.

01 · What Is Charge, Really?

It was never the orbiting. The charge was always there.

Here's the misconception worth killing first: charge is not something an electron generates by circling a proton, the way spinning a bicycle wheel generates momentum. That mental picture comes from the old Bohr "solar system" atom — a useful cartoon, but wrong in exactly this way. An electron sitting perfectly still, with no proton anywhere nearby, still has exactly the same charge it always had. Motion has nothing to do with it.

So what is charge, then? It's best treated as an intrinsic property of certain particles — as fundamental and un-derived as mass. An electron doesn't "have" mass because it's doing something; it just has mass, permanently, as part of what makes it an electron and not some other particle. Charge works the same way. Every electron in the universe carries exactly −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, every proton carries exactly +1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, full stop — not because of what they're doing, but because of what they are.

Orbiting vs. Frozen Still
Watch the cyan halo, not the motion. That halo represents the electron's field — the region of space it pushes and pulls on. Freeze the orbit completely and the halo doesn't shrink, flicker, or change in any way. Charge is a property the electron carries with it at all times, moving or still.

The other load-bearing fact about charge is that it's quantized — it doesn't come in a smooth, infinitely divisible continuum like pouring water. It comes in fixed-size steps, all whole-number multiples of one elementary unit, e ≈ 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. You can have a charge of −2e or +5e, but never 1.5e. (Quarks, bound permanently inside protons and neutrons, carry fractional charges of e — +⅔e or −⅓e — but the strong force never lets you isolate one to measure alone; every particle you can actually pick up and hold has a whole-number charge.)

Drag the slider anywhere you like — the amber dot only ever lands on a whole-number rung. There's no such thing as a particle with 1.35 elementary charges. This snapping isn't a limitation of measurement; it's how charge actually works.
Quick check: an electron is brought to a complete standstill, with no motion at all. What happens to its charge?
Charge is intrinsic — it doesn't require motion to exist, and it can't be created or destroyed, only moved around or (in pair-production/annihilation) converted between particles while the total stays conserved.
02 · Coulomb's Law

Same shape as gravity. Almost incomprehensibly stronger.

Charles-Augustin de Coulomb measured it directly in 1785: the force between two charges is proportional to each charge's size, and falls off with the square of the distance between them.

F = k · |q₁ q₂|r²k = 1 / (4πε₀) ≈ 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C² — ε₀ = permittivity of free space

Same charges (+/+ or −/−) push apart; opposite charges (+/−) pull together. Notice the exponent: double the distance and the force doesn't halve — it drops to a quarter. Triple it, and the force falls to a ninth. This inverse-square shape is exactly the same one gravity uses, which isn't a coincidence — both describe an influence spreading out over the surface of an ever-expanding sphere.

Both positive → they push apart. Flip either slider negative and watch the arrows reverse to point inward — opposite charges always attract. Notice this is a mutual force too: by Newton's third law, q₁ pushes q₂ exactly as hard as q₂ pushes q₁, just in opposite directions.
A sense of scale: the reason gravity feels like the dominant force in daily life — even though it's astronomically weaker per particle — is that ordinary matter is almost perfectly electrically neutral, with equal positive and negative charge cancelling out at short range. Strip that cancellation away, even slightly, and electric forces overwhelm gravity instantly. A single bolt of lightning is charge imbalance winning.
You slide q₂ so the distance r doubles, changing nothing else. What happens to the force?
F ∝ 1/r² — an inverse-square law. Doubling r means dividing the force by 2² = 4, regardless of how big or small the charges are.
a charge doesn't wait for company to push — fields & potential
03 · The Electric Field

Force, pre-divided down to a property of empty space.

Instead of asking "what force does charge A put on charge B," it's more powerful to split the question in two. First: what does charge A do to the space around it, regardless of who or what ever shows up? That's the electric field, E. Second, whenever some other charge q happens to sit at a point in that space, the force on it is simply F = qE at that location.

E = F / qfield = force per unit of (positive) test charge, measured in N/C — identical to V/m

That division by q is the whole trick. Bring a huge test charge into the field and it feels a huge force; bring a tiny one and it feels a tiny force — but divide either force by its own charge, and you get back the exact same number both times. That number belongs to the source charge and the location, not to whatever you used to probe it. It's the same logic as asking "how steep is this hill" instead of "how hard did that specific rock roll" — steepness is a property of the hill; rolling force depends on the rock too.

Why define the field as E = F/q instead of just talking about the force directly?
Force always depends on two charges. Dividing by the test charge's own size leaves behind a quantity that depends only on the source charge and where you're standing — reusable for any test charge you might bring in next.
04 · Electric Potential

Height, but for charge instead of gravity.

If the field E is like the steepness of a hill at each point, the electric potential V is the height itself. It's defined as potential energy per unit charge, V = U/q, and for a point charge it works out to V = kQ/r — highest right next to a positive charge, falling off as you move away. Drop a positive test charge anywhere near a positive source and, exactly like a ball on a real hill, it naturally accelerates away, toward lower potential — because that's the direction that releases stored energy.

V = k · Qr     U = qV V in volts (joules per coulomb) — a scalar, no direction, unlike E

If "always move toward lower V" sounds like gradient descent from optimization — it's the exact same math. E = −dV/dr is nothing more than "which way is downhill, and how steep."
A positive test charge is released near a negative source charge. Which way does it move, and why?
Near a negative charge, V is deeply negative and climbs toward zero as you move away — so the "downhill" direction (toward lower V) is actually inward, toward the source. That's the potential-energy picture of attraction.
05 · Gauss's Law

Count what's inside. Ignore everything about the shape.

Gauss's Law says the total electric flux — loosely, "how much field pokes through" — leaving any closed surface depends only on the net charge sealed up inside it. Not the surface's shape, not its size, not anything about charges sitting outside. Picture a running tap sealed inside a balloon: however you deform that balloon — sphere, potato, star-shaped — the total flow rate measured leaving its surface is identical, because it's all coming from one place: the tap.

∮ E · dA = Qencε₀total flux through any closed surface = enclosed charge ÷ permittivity of free space

The payoff: for a highly symmetric charge distribution — a sphere, an infinite line, an infinite sheet — you can choose a Gaussian surface that matches the symmetry, and E pops out algebraically in one line, with zero calculus. It's the reason you can quote the field of a charged sphere or a charged plate from memory instead of re-deriving it from Coulomb's Law every time.

06 · Electrical Ground

There's no such thing as an absolute voltage — only a difference.

You can't measure "the voltage" at a single point any more than you can measure "the altitude" of a single point without agreeing on sea level first. Every voltmeter reading is secretly a comparison between two probes. Ground is simply the node a circuit's designer picks to call 0V — after that, every other point can be labeled with one number instead of always saying "12V above the other one."

Crucially, that reference node does not need to be connected to the physical planet to deserve the name. A 9V battery circuit sitting in your hand, completely isolated from any wire touching the dirt outside, still has a perfectly good "ground" — it's just the battery's negative terminal. This is called a floating ground, and it's just as legitimate internally as one wired to an actual earth rod. "Earth ground" is a separate, more specific idea covered next.

Click Any Node to Make It "Ground"
Node C is currently "ground" (0V). Click node A or B instead, and watch every label in the circuit recompute relative to your new choice. Same physical circuit, same physical currents — only the numbers written next to each wire change. That's how arbitrary the choice of "ground" really is.
True or false: a circuit's "ground" must always be physically wired to the actual planet Earth.
A battery-powered, fully isolated circuit (think: a phone, a drone, a car's 12V system) has a perfectly good internal ground with zero physical connection to soil. "Earth ground" — an actual rod in the dirt — is a specific extra feature added for safety, not a requirement for the concept of ground itself.
07 · Earth Ground & Fault Protection

The planet as an emergency exit for stray current.

Physical earth ground earns its special status from one fact: the Earth is such a colossally large conductor that dumping charge into it, or pulling charge out, barely nudges its potential at all. That makes it an extremely stable, universally available 0V — which is why building codes bond the metal chassis of appliances to an actual earth rod through the third prong of a plug.

The real payoff shows up during a fault — say, a frayed hot wire inside a metal-cased appliance brushing against the chassis. Without a ground wire, the chassis itself becomes live at full mains voltage, waiting silently for someone to touch it and become the path to earth themselves. With a ground wire in place, the fault current has an easy, low-resistance route to earth instead — a route so much easier than through a person that it draws a massive current spike, which trips the breaker or blows the fuse in well under a second, cutting power before anyone is ever at risk.

Fault Simulator
This appliance is grounded, and nothing has gone wrong yet. Hit "Trigger a Fault" to simulate the hot wire brushing the case, then compare what happens grounded vs. not.
Ground loops, briefly: if two pieces of equipment are grounded at two physically different points, tiny currents already flowing through the earth or building wiring can create a small voltage difference between those two "zeros" — injecting hum or noise into sensitive signal lines. The usual fix is single-point grounding: tying every ground reference back to one common point instead of many scattered ones.
Why does a properly grounded chassis protect a nearby person during a fault, even though they never touch the ground wire themselves?
Current takes the path of least resistance, in proportion — most of it flows through whichever route is easiest. A dedicated ground wire is a far easier route than a human body, and the resulting current spike is exactly what trips the breaker before real danger can develop.

CHAPTER 1.3 · FROM ROCK TO RADIO

Every magnet is just
tiny electrons agreeing.

Magnetism isn't a separate force from electricity — it's the same force, seen from a different angle. Why magnets work, how current creates magnetism, and the one equation (the Lorentz force) that ties it all together and explains every motor, speaker, and CRT ever built.

01 · Why Magnets Work at All

A magnet is billions of tiny compass needles, all pointing the same way.

Every electron spinning inside every atom acts like an impossibly tiny magnet, with its own north and south pole — a property called spin. In most materials, neighboring atoms' tiny magnetic fields point in random directions and cancel each other out completely. In materials like iron, cobalt, and nickel, though, groups of atoms called magnetic domains naturally line up with their neighbors. An unmagnetized piece of iron still has all these domains — they're just pointing every which way, like a crowd of compass needles scattered randomly, so their fields cancel out on average. Magnetize it, and every domain snaps into the same direction — suddenly billions of tiny fields point one way and add up into one strong field you can feel.

Domain Alignment
Every arrow above is a simplified magnetic domain. Aligned, they reinforce each other into one strong net field — exactly what happens when you stroke a steel needle repeatedly with a magnet to make your own compass.
02 · Magnetic Field Lines

Invisible loops that always run from North back to South.

A magnetic field is easiest to picture as a set of loops flowing out of the North pole, curving through the space around the magnet, and diving back in at the South pole — then continuing right through the inside of the magnet to close the loop. A compass needle anywhere nearby just rotates to align with whatever field line passes through it, exactly like a leaf caught in a stream orients itself with the current.

Compass Around a Bar Magnet
Every compass points along the local field direction. Notice they all curve from N around to S — never the other way, and never crossing each other.
03 · Electromagnetism

Moving charge doesn't just flow — it radiates a magnetic field for free.

Here's the deep connection: any moving electric charge creates a magnetic field around itself, automatically, as a direct consequence of relativity acting on electric fields (the full explanation is genuinely relativistic, but the everyday rule of thumb is simpler). Current flowing through a straight wire creates field loops circling around it — point your right thumb in the direction of current flow, and your curled fingers show the field's direction (the right-hand rule). Coil that wire into a loop, and all those circular fields stack up inside the loop into one strong, directional field — you've just built an electromagnet.

Right-Hand Rule: Straight Wire
Point your right thumb up (the current's direction) and your fingers curl counter-clockwise viewed from above — that's exactly the direction these field rings circle.

04 · Electromagnetic Induction (a preview)

Run the whole idea backwards: a changing field creates current.

If current creates a magnetic field, the reverse is also true — a magnetic field that's changing near a wire induces a voltage in that wire (Faraday's Law). Critically, it's the change that matters, not the field itself: a magnet sitting still next to a coil, no matter how strong, induces nothing at all. Only a magnet moving relative to the coil — its field strength at the coil changing moment to moment — pushes electrons into motion. This is the entire principle behind generators, transformers, and regenerative braking. Chapter 1.4 goes deep on this — Faraday's Law properly stated, Lenz's Law, eddy currents, and hysteresis. This is just enough to finish the story of "moving charge ↔ magnetic field" before the Lorentz force ties both directions together below.

05 · The Lorentz Force

The one equation that explains every motor, speaker, and CRT ever built.

A charged particle moving through a magnetic field feels a force — but a strange one: it doesn't push along the direction of motion or along the field, it pushes perpendicular to both at once. This is the Lorentz force:

F = qv × B

where q is the charge, v is its velocity, and B is the magnetic field. Because the force is always perpendicular to the velocity, it never speeds the particle up or slows it down — it only steers it, bending its path into a circle, like a ball on a string being swung around your head by a constant sideways pull.

Charged Particle in a Field
Release the particle and watch it curve into a circle, not a straight line. Flip the charge sign or the field direction and the circle bends the opposite way — that's the right-hand rule (or left-hand for negative charge) doing exactly what it does inside a CRT tube or a mass spectrometer.
This same equation, unmodified, is what steers electron beams in an old CRT television (6.3), what focuses a mass spectrometer, and what confines plasma in a fusion reactor. One cross product, four very different machines.
06 · The Motor Principle

Zoom out from one charge to a whole wire, and you get a motor.

A current-carrying wire is just billions of moving charges, so the Lorentz force applies to the whole wire at once — the field pushes the entire wire sideways with a force F = BIL (field × current × wire length). This is the exact mechanism spinning every electric motor that exists — from a desk fan (6.7) to an EV drivetrain: current in a wire sitting inside a magnetic field feels a sideways shove, and if that wire is wound around a rotor, the shove becomes rotation.

Force on a Current-Carrying Wire
Flip the current direction and watch the force arrow flip too — this single reversal, timed by the inverter in 2.3, is what keeps a motor's rotor continuously pushed in the direction it's already spinning.
Quick Check
A charged particle moves in a straight line with no magnetic field present. A field is then switched on, aligned exactly parallel to its velocity. What happens?
Right — the Lorentz force depends on v × B, and the cross product of two parallel vectors is zero. A charge moving exactly along the field feels no force at all; only the velocity component perpendicular to the field gets deflected.

Electricity and magnetism were never two separate things.

Moving charge makes a field. A changing field pushes charge. Every motor, generator, speaker, and transformer later in this bible is just this one relationship, wired up in a particular shape.
