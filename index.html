Electricity, actually
explained.

Not "V = IR, memorize it." You're going to watch voltage, current and resistance happen — drag sliders, flip switches, and see the numbers move because you moved them.

01 · The Big Picture

Electricity is just water in a very impatient pipe.

Before any formulas: three words you'll hear constantly are voltage, current, and resistance. Here's the only mental model you actually need: a water tower feeding a pipe. Voltage is the pressure pushing the water. Current is how much water is actually flowing past a point per second. Resistance is how narrow the pipe is — narrow the pipe, and less water gets through for the same push.

The water analogy is a mental model. Here's what these units actually mean in real physics:

A volt is energy per unit of charge. A 9V battery hands every coulomb that passes through it 9 joules to spend. An amp is literally how many coulombs go past per second.

03 · The Resistor

The component whose entire job is to say "not so fast."

A resistor doesn't block current — it converts some of the electrical energy into heat as electrons shove past a material that doesn't want to carry them easily. More resistance = more shoving = more heat = less current gets through.

Color Code Decoder
100 Ω
Power rule: P = I² × R. Double the current through a resistor and it dissipates 4× the heat.
Why it heats up
🔥

Inside that tan body is carbon or metal film — a material with just enough atoms in the way that electrons constantly bump into them. Every bump releases heat.

Resistors are rated for a maximum power. Exceed it and they literally burn — that's the smoke you sometimes see.
Quick Check
You double the resistance in a circuit but keep voltage the same. What happens to current?
Right — I = V/R. Double the denominator, halve the answer.
04 · One Path, Or Many

Wire two resistors two different ways, and they stop behaving alike.

Chain two resistors one after another and every electron must pass through both — that's series. Wire them side by side between the same two points and each electron only has to pick one — that's parallel. Same two resistors, two completely different circuits.

05 · The Capacitor

A tiny water tank that hates being empty or full.

A capacitor stores charge between two metal plates. Connect it to a voltage source and it fills up — fast at first, then slower as it gets close to full. Disconnect the source and give it a path, and it drains the same way. It never switches instantly — that lag is the entire reason capacitors exist in circuits.

Charge / Discharge
🔋
This delay is called the RC time constant. Bigger resistor or bigger capacitor = slower fill. Engineers use this to build timers and smooth out noisy power.
06 · The Inductor

A coil with the momentum of a flywheel.

An inductor is just a coil of wire, but current through a coil creates a magnetic field — and that field resists any change in current. Try to switch current on instantly and the inductor fights it, ramping up gradually. Try to switch it off instantly and it fights that too, sometimes hard enough to spark.

Flywheel Model
🔄
Watch the flywheel — it doesn't jump to speed. It winds up. That ramp-up time is the inductor "charging" its magnetic field.

CHAPTER 1.2 · FROM ROCK TO RADIO

Not orbits.
Just charge.

Every circuit you'll ever build traces back to one unexplained fact: some particles push, some pull, and it has nothing to do with them spinning around anything.

CHARGE · FIELD · POTENTIAL · GAUSS'S LAW · GROUND
What Is Charge? Coulomb's Law Electric Field Potential Gauss's Law Ground
01 · What Is Charge, Really?

It was never the orbiting. The charge was always there.

Here's the misconception worth killing first: charge is not something an electron generates by circling a proton. An electron sitting perfectly still, with no proton anywhere nearby, still has exactly the same charge it always had. Motion has nothing to do with it.

Charge is best treated as an intrinsic property of certain particles — as fundamental as mass. Every electron carries exactly −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, every proton carries exactly +1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, full stop — not because of what they're doing, but because of what they are.

Frozen Still
⚛️

The electron's charge is intrinsic. It doesn't require motion to exist.

Charge is quantized. It comes in whole-number multiples of e ≈ 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. You can have +2e or −5e, but never 1.5e.
02 · Coulomb's Law

Same shape as gravity. Almost incomprehensibly stronger.

F = k · |q₁ q₂|r² k ≈ 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²

Same charges push apart; opposite charges pull together. Double the distance and the force drops to a quarter. Triple it, and the force falls to a ninth.

03 · The Electric Field

Force, pre-divided down to a property of empty space.

E = F / q field = force per unit of positive test charge, in N/C or V/m

Instead of asking "what force does charge A put on charge B," split the question in two. First: what does charge A do to the space around it? That's the electric field E. Second, whenever some other charge q sits in that space, the force on it is F = qE.

04 · Electric Potential

Height, but for charge instead of gravity.

V = k · Qr    U = qV V in volts (joules per coulomb) — a scalar

If the field E is like the steepness of a hill, the electric potential V is the height itself. It's potential energy per unit charge. Drop a positive test charge near a positive source and it accelerates away, toward lower potential — exactly like a ball rolling downhill.

06 · Electrical Ground

There's no such thing as an absolute voltage — only a difference.

You can't measure "the voltage" at a single point any more than you can measure "the altitude" of a single point without agreeing on sea level first. Ground is simply the node a circuit's designer picks to call 0V.

Crucially, that reference node does not need to be connected to the physical planet. A 9V battery circuit sitting in your hand has a perfectly good "ground" — it's just the battery's negative terminal. This is called a floating ground.

CHAPTER 1.3 · FROM ROCK TO RADIO

Every magnet is just
tiny electrons agreeing.

Magnetism isn't a separate force from electricity — it's the same force, seen from a different angle. Why magnets work, how current creates magnetism, and the Lorentz force that explains every motor ever built.

DOMAINS · FIELD LINES · RIGHT-HAND RULE · F = qv×B · MOTORS
Why Magnets Work Field Lines Electromagnetism Lorentz Force Motor Principle
01 · Why Magnets Work at All

A magnet is billions of tiny compass needles, all pointing the same way.

Every electron spinning inside every atom acts like an impossibly tiny magnet with its own north and south pole — a property called spin. In most materials, neighboring atoms' tiny magnetic fields point in random directions and cancel out. In iron, cobalt, and nickel, groups of atoms called magnetic domains naturally line up with their neighbors. Magnetize it, and every domain snaps into the same direction — suddenly billions of tiny fields add up into one strong field you can feel.

Domain Alignment
⬆️⬆️⬆️⬆️⬆️⬆️
Every arrow is a magnetic domain. Aligned, they reinforce into one strong net field.
02 · Magnetic Field Lines

Invisible loops that always run from North back to South.

A magnetic field is a set of loops flowing out of the North pole, curving through space, and diving back in at the South pole — then continuing through the inside of the magnet to close the loop. A compass needle anywhere nearby just rotates to align with whatever field line passes through it.

Compass Around a Bar Magnet
🧲
Every compass points along the local field direction. They all curve from N around to S — never crossing each other.
03 · Electromagnetism

Moving charge doesn't just flow — it radiates a magnetic field for free.

Any moving electric charge creates a magnetic field around itself, automatically. Current flowing through a straight wire creates field loops circling around it — point your right thumb in the direction of current flow, and your curled fingers show the field's direction (the right-hand rule).

Right-Hand Rule
⬆️ CURRENT FLOW
Point your right thumb in the current direction — your fingers curl the way the field circles.

2.0×
More current, more turns, or an iron core — all three make the field stronger.
04 · The Lorentz Force

The one equation that explains every motor, speaker, and CRT ever built.

F = qv × B force = charge × velocity × magnetic field (cross product)

A charged particle moving through a magnetic field feels a force perpendicular to both its velocity and the field. It never speeds the particle up or slows it down — it only steers it, bending its path into a circle.

Charged Particle in a Field
⚡
Release the particle and watch it curve into a circle. Flip the charge sign or the field direction and the circle bends the opposite way.
05 · The Motor Principle

Zoom out from one charge to a whole wire, and you get a motor.

A current-carrying wire is just billions of moving charges, so the Lorentz force applies to the whole wire at once — the field pushes the entire wire sideways with a force F = BIL (field × current × wire length). This is the exact mechanism spinning every electric motor that exists.

Force on a Wire
⬆️ CURRENT → ➡️ FORCE
Flip the current direction and the force flips too — this reversal is what keeps a motor's rotor spinning.
END OF CHAPTER 1.3

Electricity and magnetism were never two separate things.

01 · The Biot-Savart Law

Build a magnetic field, one tiny current-piece at a time.

dB = μ₀4π · I dl × r̂r² contribution from one tiny segment dl carrying current I

Just like Coulomb's Law describes the field from a single point charge, the Biot-Savart Law describes the field from a single tiny piece of current-carrying wire. The contribution points perpendicular to both the wire and the line to your observation point — governed by the right-hand rule.

02 · How Materials Respond to a Field

Every material reacts to a magnetic field. Almost none of them agree on how.

Diamagnetism is present in every material — an applied field induces tiny opposing circulation in each atom's electron cloud, giving a faint, universal repulsion. It's usually too weak to notice.

Paramagnetism shows up in materials whose atoms already carry a small magnetic moment. Apply a field, and those moments partially tip toward alignment — a weak attraction, constantly fighting thermal chaos.

Ferromagnetism is paramagnetism's louder cousin: neighboring atomic moments lock onto each other through the "exchange interaction," forming domains even with no field applied. An external field grows the favorably-aligned domains at the expense of the others.

03 · Curie's Law & the Curie Temperature

Heat scrambles alignment. Cool it, and order returns.

For paramagnets, Curie's Law makes the temperature dependence explicit: χ = C/T — susceptibility falls off inversely with absolute temperature, because hotter atoms jostle around more violently.

Ferromagnets have a dramatic threshold: below the Curie temperature Tc (770°C for iron), the exchange interaction keeps domains locked in alignment. Push above Tc, and thermal chaos finally overwhelms it — domains scramble and spontaneous magnetization vanishes.

04 · Permeability

Same coil, same current — a very different field, depending what's inside it.

Permeability, μ = μ₀μᵣ, measures how strongly a material "welcomes" magnetic field lines. Wind a coil around an iron core instead of air, and the internal field B = μH gets amplified by that core's μᵣ — sometimes by a factor of thousands.

CHAPTER 1.5 · FROM ROCK TO RADIO

Nature refuses to let
flux change for free.

A changing magnetic field writing a voltage into anything nearby, and the universe always making sure that voltage fights back against whatever caused it.

FARADAY'S LAW · LENZ'S LAW · EDDY CURRENTS · HYSTERESIS

01 · Faraday's Law

A changing flux writes a voltage into any nearby loop.

Flux, Φ = B·A·cos θ, measures how much magnetic field passes through a loop. Faraday's Law says any change in that flux induces an EMF (voltage) around the loop — critically, it's the rate of change that matters, not the field strength itself.

EMF = −dΦdtinduced voltage = negative rate of change of flux through the loop

02 · Lenz's Law

The induced current always fights back.

Lenz's Law is the minus sign in Faraday's equation, made physically vivid: the induced current always flows in the direction that opposes the change that created it. A magnet approaching a coil induces a current whose own field pushes back, repelling the approach.

Approach vs. Retreat
Push the magnet toward the coil, then pull it away — watch the force reverse direction. Stop moving, and both vanish: no change, no induced current.
03 · Eddy Currents

Lenz's Law, but the "coil" is just a solid chunk of metal.

Drop a magnet through a solid conductive pipe, and the bulk metal itself hosts countless overlapping circulating currents, called eddy currents. Those currents dissipate real energy as heat and create a drag force that opposes the motion.

04 · Hysteresis

Ferromagnetic materials remember where they've been.

Sweep an external field H up and down through a ferromagnetic material and plot the resulting internal field B — it doesn't retrace the same curve. The loop's enclosed area is real energy, lost as heat, every single cycle.

"Soft" materials trace a thin loop with little energy loss — ideal for transformer cores. "Hard" materials trace a fat loop — exactly what makes a permanent magnet stay magnetized.

A crystal that can't
quite decide if it conducts.

Every transistor on Earth is built on a material that's neither a proper conductor nor a proper insulator — and that indecision, tuned on purpose, is the entire trick.

BAND THEORY · DOPING · N-TYPE / P-TYPE · WHY COLD CHIPS STILL WORK
Energy Bands Doping Why Chips Work in the Cold
01 · Energy Bands

Every electron in a crystal is stuck on one of two floors.

Pack atoms into a crystal and their individual, sharp electron energy levels smear together into two big allowed "floors," separated by a forbidden gap: the valence band and the conduction band. Whether a material conducts depends almost entirely on how wide that forbidden gap is.

02 · Doping: N-Type & P-Type

Sprinkle in the right impurity, and conduction stops being rare.

Doping means deliberately contaminating the silicon crystal with a tiny trace of a different element. Swap in phosphorus (5 valence electrons) and each dopant atom contributes one extra electron — this is n-type silicon. Swap in boron (3 valence electrons) and each dopant atom leaves a bond short an electron — a mobile hole — giving p-type silicon.

Donor / Acceptor Levels
A donor electron only has to hop a tiny distance to join the conduction band — not the full 1.12 eV gap. At almost any temperature, essentially every donor atom is already ionized.
03 · The Cold-Weather Question

Pure silicon nearly stops conducting when cold. No real chip depends on that.

Pure silicon really is a near-perfect insulator at absolute zero, but no chip relies on pure silicon conducting. Every transistor's silicon is deliberately doped, and doped silicon's carriers come almost entirely from ionizing those shallow donor/acceptor levels — not from rare thermal leaps across the full intrinsic gap.

CHAPTER 2.1 · FROM ROCK TO RADIO

Fixed polarity.
Polarity that flips.

DC is a battery: + is always + and − is always −. AC is the wall socket: the terminals swap which one is + and which is − dozens of times a second.

POLARITY · FREQUENCY · RMS · WHY AC WON THE GRID
Fixed vs Switching Polarity Anatomy of a Sine Wave RMS Why AC Won the Grid
01 · Fixed vs Switching Polarity

The entire difference, before any math: which way is "forward"?

Direct Current (DC) comes from a source where one terminal is permanently + and the other is permanently −. Alternating Current (AC) comes from a source whose terminals swap which one is + and which is − on a regular schedule.

Same Wire, Two Behaviors
DC: electrons drift steadily one way. Switch to AC and watch them rock back and forth in place, never actually going anywhere on average.
02 · Anatomy of a Sine Wave

Four numbers describe the entire shape.

V(t) = Vpeak · sin(2πft)

Mains AC traces a sine wave. Four quantities describe it: amplitude (peak height), period T (seconds per full cycle), frequency f = 1/T (Hertz), and phase.

Live Waveform
03 · RMS — the "Effective" Value
VRMS = Vpeak√2 ≈ 0.707 × Vpeak

US mains is quoted as 120V, but the sine wave's actual peak is about 170V. RMS is defined so that an AC source delivers the same average heating power to a resistor as a DC source at that same voltage.

04 · Why AC Won the Grid

Not because AC is "better" — because it's easier to change.

Power lost in a transmission wire is Ploss = I²R. A transformer steps voltage up and down for AC cheaply with no moving parts. This was the deciding advantage AC held over Edison's DC in the late 1800s.

Power electronics have since gotten good enough that long-distance HVDC links are now used for certain jobs, but the everyday grid feeding your outlets is still built on AC.

Same component.
Two completely different jobs.

A resistor doesn't care whether it's fed DC or AC — but almost nothing else in a circuit can say that. Capacitors, inductors, diodes, transistors, and op-amps all behave differently depending on whether the signal sits still or wiggles.

IMPEDANCE · REACTANCE · PHASE · RECTIFICATION · BIASING · SLEW RATE
01 · Every Component, AC vs. DC

Resistance is one number. Impedance is a whole relationship.

X_C = 12πfC     X_L = 2πfL capacitive reactance falls with frequency · inductive reactance rises with frequency
The Component Browser

DC: V = IR, always. AC: exactly the same — no phase shift, no frequency dependence.

A resistor is the one component that treats DC and AC identically.

DC: a fully charged capacitor blocks current completely. AC: it charges and discharges every cycle, so current does flow, more easily at higher frequency — and it leads voltage by 90°.

DC: once current stabilizes, an inductor is just the resistance of its own wire. AC: it resists any change in current, more strongly at higher frequency — and voltage leads current by 90°.

DC: a simple one-way gate — conducts above ~0.7V forward, blocks reverse. AC: used as a rectifier, chopping an alternating signal down to one polarity.

DC: sets the bias point — where on the device's transfer curve it's sitting at rest. AC: a small signal riding on top of that bias point gets amplified according to the local steepness of the curve.

DC: a precision comparison/amplification tool. AC: gain rolls off past the gain-bandwidth product, and fast, large swings can outrun the op-amp's slew rate.

Parallel sends every bit of a byte at once. Serial sends bits one after another on a single wire.

Two wires (TX and RX), no shared clock line. Both sides agree on a speed (the baud rate) in advance.

Two wires — SDA and SCL — shared by every device. A transaction starts with a START condition, followed by a 7-bit address.

Works on the same core idea as I2C's addressing, but scaled up to a whole factory floor — RS-485 or Ethernet. A single master polls each device by address.
