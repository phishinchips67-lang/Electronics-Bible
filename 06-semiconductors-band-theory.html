CHAPTER 1.6 · FROM ROCK TO RADIO

A crystal that can't
quite decide if it conducts.

Every transistor on Earth is built on a material that's neither a proper conductor nor a proper insulator — and that indecision, tuned on purpose, is the entire trick. This chapter also settles a genuinely great question: if pure silicon barely conducts at room temperature, how does a laptop even turn on in a Siberian winter?

01 · Energy Bands

Every electron in a crystal is stuck on one of two floors.

Pack atoms into a crystal and their individual, sharp electron energy levels smear together into two big allowed "floors," separated by a forbidden gap: the valence band (electrons bound to atoms, going nowhere) and the conduction band (electrons free to roam and carry current). Whether a material conducts depends almost entirely on how wide that forbidden gap is, and how full each floor already is.

CONDUCTOR
no gap — always conducts
SEMICONDUCTOR
small gap — sometimes conducts
INSULATOR
huge gap — never conducts

Silicon's gap (1.12 eV) is small enough that ordinary heat can occasionally kick an electron across it. At absolute zero, no electron has any thermal energy at all — the valence band is completely full, the conduction band completely empty, and pure silicon behaves exactly like an insulator. Warm it to room temperature, and a small fraction of electrons pick up enough random thermal energy to leap the gap, each leaving behind a "hole" in the valence band — giving pure (intrinsic) silicon its famously weak, temperature-hungry conductivity.

With zero thermal energy available, the valence band stays completely full and the conduction band stays completely empty — textbook insulator behavior. This is real, and it's the starting point for the cold-weather-chip question in Section 03.
02 · Doping: N-Type & P-Type

Sprinkle in the right impurity, and conduction stops being rare.

Doping means deliberately contaminating the silicon crystal with a tiny trace of a different element — often as little as one dopant atom per million silicon atoms. Swap in phosphorus (5 valence electrons, one more than silicon's 4) and each dopant atom contributes one extra electron that's only loosely tied to its parent atom — this is n-type silicon. Swap in boron (3 valence electrons, one fewer) and each dopant atom leaves a bond short an electron — a mobile hole — giving p-type silicon.

The reason this matters so much: those donor and acceptor states sit in energy-level terms extremely close to the band edges — roughly 0.01 to 0.05 eV away, compared to silicon's full 1.12 eV band gap. It takes barely any thermal energy at all to ionize them.

A donor electron only has to hop this tiny distance to join the conduction band — not the full 1.12 eV gap intrinsic carriers face. At almost any temperature worth mentioning, essentially every donor atom is already ionized.
A donor level sits only ~0.045 eV below the conduction band, versus silicon's full 1.12 eV gap. What does that imply about donor electrons at room temperature?
This ~25× smaller ionization energy is the whole reason doped silicon behaves so differently from pure silicon — and it's the key fact the next section uses to answer the cold-weather question.
03 · The Cold-Weather Question

Pure silicon nearly stops conducting when cold. No real chip depends on that.

So here's the paradox, stated properly: pure silicon really is a near-perfect insulator at absolute zero, and only weakly conductive at room temperature — that part of the premise is completely correct. But no chip in a laptop, phone, or car actually relies on pure silicon conducting. Every transistor's silicon is deliberately doped, and doped silicon's carriers come almost entirely from ionizing those shallow donor/acceptor levels from Section 02 — not from rare thermal leaps across the full intrinsic gap.

Because that ionization energy is so small, doped silicon's carrier concentration is essentially flat — nearly independent of temperature — across an enormous range that comfortably covers every weather condition Earth produces. Room temperature — the doped curve is flat here, and the pure-silicon curve is negligible in comparison. This is the normal operating point for essentially every chip ever made.
The short answer: a chip's silicon isn't waiting on rare thermal excitation across a 1.12 eV gap — it's running on dopant atoms with a ~0.045 eV ionization energy that stay almost completely ionized from deep cryogenic temperatures up to well past anything the weather can produce. A Siberian winter, even the coldest ones, is nowhere near cold enough to "freeze out" a properly doped semiconductor.
Bonus: cold can actually help. Lower temperature means less thermal vibration in the crystal lattice (less "phonon scattering"), so electrons and holes can often move more freely — carrier mobility tends to improve in the cold, up to a point. This is part of why deliberately-cooled "cryo-CMOS" is an active research area for high-performance and quantum-adjacent computing, not despite the cold, but partly because of it.

So what does go wrong with electronics in the cold? Almost never the silicon. It's usually: lithium-ion batteries (the chemistry itself slows dramatically, cutting available current and capacity), LCD screens (the liquid crystal fluid stiffens and responds sluggishly), condensation (bringing a cold device into a warm, humid room), and plain mechanical stress on solder joints and connectors from repeated thermal contraction and expansion.

Why doesn't a laptop's silicon stop working in a brutal winter, given that pure silicon is nearly an insulator when cold?
The intrinsic 1.12 eV gap is real and matters for pure silicon, but it's almost irrelevant to a working transistor, which runs on the deliberately engineered, far smaller ionization energy of its dopants instead.
