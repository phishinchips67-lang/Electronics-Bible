CHAPTER 3.3 · FROM ROCK TO RADIO

The chip that's obsessed
with two wires being equal.

The op-amp is arguably the single most useful chip ever made — and its entire personality boils down to one obsession: make both inputs match, at any cost.

01 · Open-Loop Behavior

A referee with a hair-trigger.

An op-amp has two inputs — V+ and V− — and one output. Left to its own devices (called open loop), it does something almost violent: it compares the two inputs and, even for a difference of microvolts, slams the output all the way to one power rail or the other. It behaves less like an amplifier here and more like a judge that only ever says "V+ wins" or "V− wins" — nothing in between.

02 · Negative Feedback

Tame the beast by feeding its own output back in.

A hair-trigger judge is useless for amplifying an audio signal smoothly. The trick that makes op-amps actually useful is negative feedback: wire a resistor from the output back to the V− input. Now the op-amp isn't just comparing — it's constantly adjusting its output to keep V− chasing V+, like a thermostat that keeps nudging the heater to hold room temperature exactly at the target, never overshooting into all-or-nothing territory. The resistor ratio sets a precise, controllable gain.

03 · The Voltage Follower

Same voltage out — but now with muscle.

Wire the output straight back into V− with zero resistors at all (gain = 1), and the op-amp just copies V+ to the output exactly. Pointless? Not quite — a weak signal source (like a sensor) often can't supply real current without its own voltage sagging, the way a garden hose trickle collapses the moment you try to fill a bucket fast. The follower acts like a bodyguard: it repeats the signal faithfully but supplies all the current itself, so the fragile source is never actually loaded down.

Loaded Source: With vs Without a Buffer
With the buffer: the op-amp supplies the load's current itself, so the source's 3.0V reaches the load untouched. The source barely feels the load is even there.
04 · The Summing Amplifier

An audio mixer's real circuit, one op-amp deep.

Feed several input signals into V− through their own resistors, keep one feedback resistor, and the op-amp's obsession with keeping V− at zero forces it to output the sum of all those inputs (inverted). This is the literal circuit inside a mixing console channel strip — three microphones in, one combined signal out, and each input's resistor can even set its own volume level.

05 · Where You'll Actually Meet This Chip

Three jobs, one part number.

The same op-amp, wired three different ways, quietly runs a huge chunk of the analog world:

Comparator Mode

Open-loop, no feedback. A night-light circuit compares an LDR's voltage against a fixed reference — the instant it dips below the threshold at dusk, the output slams high and switches on an LED.

Buffer Mode

Gain of 1, full feedback. A pH probe or piezo sensor produces a signal too weak to drive anything — a buffer stage protects the reading before it reaches an ADC.

Amplifier Mode

Set gain with RfRin. A microphone preamp takes a few millivolts from a mic capsule and boosts it to line level before it hits a speaker or recorder.

Quick Check
What actually changes an op-amp from a "slam to the rails" comparator into a smooth, controlled amplifier?
Exactly — feedback is what turns the op-amp's raw, huge open-loop gain into a tame, resistor-controlled gain you can actually design with.
