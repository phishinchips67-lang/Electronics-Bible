CHAPTER 4.1 · FROM ROCK TO RADIO

From "yes or no"
to remembering.

A logic gate answers one instant question about its inputs. Wire two gates into a loop and something new appears that no single gate has: memory. This chapter builds that up from scratch, then shows where a running program actually lives.

LOGIC GATES · FLIP-FLOPS · FLASH / SRAM / EEPROM
Logic Gates Flip-Flops Flash, SRAM & EEPROM
01 · Logic Gates

Every decision a computer ever makes reduces to five of these.

A logic gate is a tiny circuit — a handful of transistors (3.2) — that outputs a single HIGH or LOW based purely on its inputs, right now, with no memory of the past. AND only outputs 1 if every input is 1. OR outputs 1 if any input is 1. NOT simply flips its one input. XOR outputs 1 only when its inputs disagree. Combine a handful of these and you can build anything from a calculator to a CPU — and combined a very specific way, as the next section shows, they can even remember.

02 · Flip-Flops

The circuit that remembers what just happened.

Every gate above reacts to the present, instantly, with no history. A flip-flop is different — it's built from a couple of cross-wired gates that hold their state even after the input that caused it disappears, like a light switch versus a doorbell button: the button only does something while you're pressing it, the switch stays wherever you last left it. This is literally what "memory" means at the transistor level. There are four flavors — try each one below.

How It's Actually Built, Gate by Gate

Every flip-flop above is built from the same tiny trick: two NOR gates, cross-wired so each one's output feeds the other's input. That feedback loop is what creates memory out of ordinary logic gates that otherwise have no memory at all.

This cross-feedback is the entire secret. Whichever gate's output goes low locks the other gate's output high, and that high output loops back to keep the first gate low — a stable state that persists with zero clock, zero memory chip, just two gates holding hands.
03 · Flash, SRAM & EEPROM

Three memories, three completely different jobs.

Flash is non-volatile — it keeps its contents with zero power — and holds your compiled program itself, the exact bytes the CPU fetches every cycle. It's written in whole blocks (erase, then write) and wears out after roughly 10,000–100,000 erase cycles, which is why you don't rewrite it constantly. SRAM is volatile — it forgets everything the instant power drops — and holds your program's working data: variables, the call stack, anything that changes while running. It's fast, freely rewritable without limit, but usually far smaller than flash. EEPROM, where present, is non-volatile like flash but rewritable byte-by-byte, reserved for small amounts of data that need to survive a power cycle and change occasionally — a WiFi password, a calibration constant.

Cut the power. Watch SRAM's contents vanish into random noise, while Flash and EEPROM come back exactly as they were.
A variable's value is lost every time the microcontroller loses power, but the program itself always comes back correctly. Where does each most likely live?
This split is universal across essentially every microcontroller: code that must survive power loss goes in Flash, working data that's expected to reset each run goes in SRAM.
